Comparative Assessment of EV Infrastructure Scaling and Ownership Economics in Vietnam
Infrastructure Paradigms: VinFast V-Green vs. Tesla Supercharger
Charging Infrastructure Expansion Timelines
The velocity of charging network deployment is a critical factor in driving consumer transition to battery electric vehicles (BEVs). In Vietnam, V-Green Global Charging Station Development JSC, established by Vingroup founder Pham Nhat Vuong, has executed a rapid, capital-intensive infrastructure rollout [cite: 1, 2, 3]. By the end of 2025, V-Green completed planning for 150,000 charging ports across Vietnam, covering both passenger cars and electric scooters [cite: 4, 5]. According to V-Green leadership, the company built out this network of more than 150,000 active charging points by March 2025 [cite: 6]. Looking to 2028, V-Green aims to scale this footprint to 500,000 active ports nationwide [cite: 1, 4, 6]. To support this rapid expansion, the company initiated a VND 10 trillion (approximately $400 million) investment phase to deploy 99 ultra-fast charging hubs along highways across 34 provinces [cite: 1, 4, 5]. Each of these highway hubs is engineered to support up to 100 charging points with a capacity of 150 kW, enabling 15-minute charging sessions [cite: 4, 5]. These hubs utilize wind and solar power paired with VinFast-manufactured Battery Energy Storage Systems (BESS) to manage grid demand [cite: 4, 5].
This compressed deployment timeline contrasts with the historical expansion of the Tesla Supercharger network. Tesla launched its proprietary network on September 24, 2012, with six active stations in California [cite: 7]. The network expanded gradually over its first decade, focusing on major driving corridors [cite: 7, 8]. It took Tesla six years to scale from those six initial stations to more than 12,000 connectors globally, and eleven years to cross the 50,000-connector milestone [cite: 7, 8]. By mid-2026, Tesla’s global infrastructure had grown to over 80,000 active Superchargers [cite: 9]. In the United States alone, the network reached 3,088 stations with 37,428 individual ports, representing 52% of all domestic DC fast-charging capacity [cite: 9].
To accelerate its network growth, Tesla reduced its capital expenditure requirements. By late 2023, Tesla was building Supercharger stalls at approximately half the cost of its competitors [cite: 7]. This efficiency was highlighted in 2025 by a disclosed capital cost of under $45,000 per stall for V3 Supercharger equipment in North America [cite: 7]. This cost structure allowed Tesla to continue expanding its network even as it reduced overall capital allocation to the division [cite: 7].

Strategic Ecosystem Moats and Universal Access Shifts
V-Green’s rapid domestic buildout serves as a core competitive moat for VinFast in Vietnam [cite: 12, 13]. Currently, V-Green stations are exclusive to VinFast vehicles, with no immediate plans to open access to rival brands [cite: 1, 12]. This exclusivity creates a substantial barrier to entry for competing manufacturers [cite: 13]. Because rival automakers cannot offer a comparable nationwide charging network, purchasing a non-VinFast EV involves significant operational compromises for consumers [cite: 13].
This closed-ecosystem strategy directly contrasts with Tesla's transition toward open-access infrastructure. While Tesla maintained exclusivity throughout its early expansion to drive vehicle sales, the company strategically pivoted to transform the Supercharger network into a universal public utility [cite: 8]. This shift was formalized by standardizing the North American Charging Standard (NACS) as SAE J3400 in late 2024 [cite: 7]. By early 2026, major global automakers integrated NACS natively or via adapters, and over 27,500 Supercharger stalls were opened to non-Tesla EVs globally [cite: 9].
In Vietnam, the regulatory framework is evolving to address this fragmentation. The Ministry of Industry and Trade, alongside the Ministry of Science and Technology, was tasked with establishing national technical standards for charging stations by late 2026 to govern grid connections, metering, and safety [cite: 1]. Concurrently, the Ministry of Construction is developing requirements for charging infrastructure in apartment buildings and public parking facilities [cite: 1]. Over time, these standards may pressure closed networks to establish interoperability, similar to the transition observed in Western markets.
The operational efficiency of a charging network also depends on managing station utilization [cite: 14]. Data from Tesla reveals that average Sessions per Stall per Day (SSD) doubled from mid-2021 to June 2025, rising to an average of over 8 sessions per day globally [cite: 14]. During this same four-year period, Tesla expanded its global physical charger count threefold, demonstrating that network scaling can occur alongside rising station utilization [cite: 14].
As of June 2025, regional utilization rates stood at 10 SSD in North America, 7 SSD in Europe, and 5.3 SSD in the Asia-Pacific region (excluding China) [cite: 14]. Managing these utilization levels is critical to maintaining network reliability and reducing wait times for consumers [cite: 14].

BYD modified its initial industrial strategy by halting plans for a proposed $250 million domestic assembly plant in Vietnam, focusing instead on scaling production hubs in Indonesia, Thailand, and Cambodia to supply regional markets [cite: 17].
BYD launched its commercial operations with 13 retail outlets, aiming to expand its distribution network to 100 dealerships across Vietnam by 2026 [cite: 18, 19, 20]. The initial model lineup targets key automotive segments, positioning imported Chinese models against established internal combustion engine (ICE) vehicles and domestic VinFast models [cite: 17, 19].

Charging Strategy and Reliance on Third-Party Networks
In the absence of a proprietary charging network in Vietnam, BYD relies on a decentralized, multi-brand strategy [cite: 28, 29]. This approach depends on third-party Charge Point Operators (CPOs) and strategic retail partnerships [cite: 28, 30]. BYD established a primary partnership with Futa EV Power (under the FUTA Group), which integrates charging stations into bus terminals, transit stops, and highway rest facilities [cite: 1, 12, 28]. BYD also leverages regional agreements with operators like Charge+, which is deploying fast-charging corridors across Southeast Asia, and local CPOs such as TMT EGreen, EV One, Esky, Eboost, and Rabbit EVC [cite: 1, 28, 29, 30].
To consolidate payment and navigation across these fragmented systems, multi-brand CPOs are increasingly integrating with aggregate platforms like the VETC electronic toll payment app [cite: 1, 30]. This app connects over 350 multi-brand stations, covering approximately half of Vietnam’s open EV charging infrastructure [cite: 1, 30].
However, relying entirely on public third-party networks introduces operational challenges. User experiences can be inconsistent, with occurrences of network offline issues and limited charger availability [cite: 1].
Additionally, the pricing structures of third-party CPOs are highly variable and utilize tiered, time-of-use pricing models [cite: 1, 12]. This contrasts with V-Green's flat-rate model of 3,858 VND/kWh for VinFast vehicles [cite: 1, 10].

VinFast relies on skateboard platform designs, utilizing both LFP and NMC battery chemistries sourced from global suppliers like CATL and Gotion [cite: 35, 36, 37].

Operational Trade-offs: Battery Subscription versus Outright Purchase
The modeling highlights a distinct financial trade-off between VinFast's battery subscription model and the traditional outright purchase model.
Capital Expenditure (CAPEX) vs. Operational Expenditure (OPEX)
The primary mechanism of the battery subscription is to lower initial vehicle acquisition costs by up to 20% depending on the model [cite: 63]. This shift lowers upfront barriers to entry [cite: 63, 64], allowing buyers to access higher-segment vehicles with a lower initial capital outlay.
However, this CAPEX reduction is offset by recurring monthly subscription fees, shifting the financial burden to long-term operational expenditures [cite: 65, 66].
Financial Impact of Subscription Tiers
The long-term financial viability of the subscription model depends heavily on monthly mileage. Under standard driving profiles (15,000 km/year), the total cost of ownership for leased vs. purchased batteries is relatively close.
For example, in Scenario A, the 5-year TCO for the VF5 Plus with a purchased battery is 576.3 Million VND, compared to 580.3 Million VND for the leased battery option [cite: 59]. This represents a nominal variance of only 4.0 Million VND over 5 years.
For the VF6 and VF7, the leased battery options are actually more cost-effective under standard mileage, saving owners 8.0 Million VND and 80.0 Million VND respectively [cite: 59].
High-Mileage Disadvantages
This balance shifts under high-mileage conditions (30,000 km/year). Because high mileage triggers VinFast's unlimited subscription tier (Gói 3), the cumulative subscription fees increase significantly over 5 years [cite: 59, 60].
In Scenario C, the VF5 Plus leased option costs 726.6 Million VND, which is 112.0 Million VND more than the outright purchase option (614.6 Million VND) [cite: 59].
For the VF7 Plus, the high-mileage leased model totals 1,464.1 Million VND, resulting in a 148.0 Million VND premium over the outright purchase option (1,316.1 Million VND) [cite: 59].
Consequently, high-mileage and commercial fleet operators achieve better long-term cost efficiencies by purchasing the battery outright.
Risk Mitigation and Depreciation
Beyond the raw TCO numbers, the battery subscription functions as a financial risk mitigation tool [cite: 63, 67]. Because VinFast retains ownership of the battery under subscription contracts, the company assumes full responsibility for battery performance [cite: 63, 64]. This includes complimentary maintenance, repairs, and free battery replacement if capacity degrades below 70% [cite: 63, 67].
Since replacement batteries can represent 20% to 30% of a vehicle's total value, this guarantee protects subscription buyers from long-term battery degradation and resale value risks [cite: 64, 67, 68].
Strategic Conclusions and Market Trajectory
The intersection of infrastructure scaling and TCO modeling highlights the competitive dynamics shaping the Vietnamese EV market. VinFast's dual-layered strategy—combining its exclusive V-Green charging network with a flexible battery subscription model—positions the domestic manufacturer to maintain a strong market position [cite: 1, 12, 13].
By controlling the physical charging infrastructure [cite: 1], V-Green mitigates range anxiety for VinFast owners while creating a high barrier to entry for foreign competitors [cite: 13, 69]. BYD and other imported brands are forced to rely on third-party public charging networks [cite: 28, 29]. This exposure introduces several operational challenges:
Fragmented User Experience: Drivers must manage multiple CPO applications, payment platforms, and varying physical interfaces to complete charging sessions [cite: 1, 30].
Dynamic Pricing Exposure: Unlike V-Green’s stable flat-rate pricing [cite: 1], third-party CPOs utilize tiered, time-of-use tariffs [cite: 1, 12]. During peak hours, these tariffs can increase charging costs by more than 100% [cite: 12, 32], significantly eroding the operating cost advantages of electric propulsion [cite: 12].
Coverage Constraints: Despite growth from CPOs like TMT EGreen and Futa EV Power [cite: 1, 30], multi-brand highway charging infrastructure remains less dense than V-Green's planned ultra-fast network [cite: 1, 4]. This limits the long-distance viability of non-VinFast vehicles [cite: 1, 29].
BYD's recent decision to register its own charging station designs indicates that the company recognizes these infrastructure bottlenecks as a primary constraint on its expansion [cite: 28]. If BYD deploys its high-power charging towers in Vietnam, it could bypass its third-party dependencies and establish a competitive, high-speed charging alternative [cite: 28].
However, deploying a nationwide charging network is a capital-intensive project [cite: 12]. Until competing infrastructure reaches scale, VinFast's V-Green network and its localized product strategy will likely serve as a dominant competitive moat, shaping the trajectory of EV adoption across Vietnam [cite: 12, 13].
