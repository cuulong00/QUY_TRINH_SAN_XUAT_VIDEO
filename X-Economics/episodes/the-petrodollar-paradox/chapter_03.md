In June 1974, United States Secretary of State Henry Kissinger traveled to Riyadh. The world was still reeling from the 1973 oil embargo. Fuel shortages had caused lines around gas stations across North America and Western Europe.

Just three years earlier, President Richard Nixon had suspended the convertibility of the dollar into gold. For the first time in modern history, the world's primary reserve currency had no tangible backing. It was backed only by the full faith and credit of the United States government. But in international trade, faith is a fragile commodity. Washington needed a physical anchor.

That anchor was black gold. On June 8, 1974, the two governments formally established the United States-Saudi Arabian Joint Commission on Economic Cooperation. The core bargain was simple, elegant, and entirely unwritten in public treaties.

The United States promised absolute military protection and advanced weaponry to the House of Saud. In return, Saudi Arabia agreed to price all of its crude oil exports exclusively in United States dollars. Furthermore, the Kingdom agreed to channel its massive oil surpluses directly back into American debt.

Through a special facility created by the Federal Reserve, the Saudi central bank purchased Treasuries through non-competitive add-on bids. This bypassed the open auction market entirely. The petrodollar recycling loop was born.

When every industrialized nation needed oil to power its factories, every nation had to acquire United States dollars. They could not pay in German marks, British pounds, or Japanese yen. They had to export physical goods to America, earn dollars, and hand those dollars over to Gulf producers. And those producers promptly reinvested that money back into United States Treasuries.

This arrangement effectively transformed Washington into an unchallengeable financial monopoly. America could print paper to buy real physical energy. The exporters then lent that paper right back to Washington at low interest rates, funding rising domestic deficits.

In recent years, internet commentators have repeated a viral claim that this agreement expired after fifty years in June 2024. That claim is entirely false. Official records from the Congressional Research Service confirm that no sunset clause ever existed. The 1974 framework was an open-ended executive partnership.

Yet the petrodollar system is facing a real crisis today. It is not dying from an expired contract. It is fracturing because the structural realities of global trade have fundamentally shifted.

For five decades, the petrodollar loop hummed without interruption. But in the spring of 2022, an unprecedented financial escalation shattered the foundation of trust that held the entire system together.
