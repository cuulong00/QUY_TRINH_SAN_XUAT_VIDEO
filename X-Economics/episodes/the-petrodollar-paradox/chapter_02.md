To understand why the world cannot simply abandon American debt, you have to understand a fundamental reality of international finance. Global commerce requires a medium of exchange. But more importantly, it requires a place to park cash overnight.

In 1960, a Belgian-American economist named Robert Triffin presented the United States Congress with a devastating observation. If the United States wanted its currency to serve as the global reserve currency, it had no choice but to supply the world with dollars. And the only way to send dollars out into the global economy is to buy more foreign goods than it sells. In plain terms, America had to run chronic, structural trade and current account deficits.

If Washington balanced its trade account and closed the import valve, the flow of dollars would freeze. International shipping would grind to a halt. Central banks would run out of liquidity. This dynamic became known as the Triffin Dilemma. The dollar cannot be both a national currency for domestic priorities and a neutral reserve asset for the entire planet. To sustain global commerce, the issuer must continuously flood the world with its liquidity.

Yet that dynamic did not end with trade. When surplus exporters and oil-producing nations accumulated mountains of dollars, they could not simply hoard paper cash. They had to reinvest those surpluses back into the United States. And the only asset market large and liquid enough to absorb trillions in global capital was United States Treasuries. This structural foreign demand depressed Washington's borrowing costs to rock-bottom levels. It handed American policymakers an exorbitant privilege: the ability to cut taxes, fund massive domestic entitlements, and accumulate a forty-trillion-dollar national debt without facing immediate market discipline.

Today, that paper forms the deepest capital market in human history. There are more than twenty-seven trillion dollars of marketable United States Treasuries in circulation. Behind closed doors, this mountain of debt serves a singular purpose. It is the pristine collateral that underpins the four-trillion-dollar-a-day global repo market.

When a commercial bank in Frankfurt lends billions to a manufacturer in Seoul, they do not exchange paper cash. They exchange short-term credit backed by United States Treasuries. No other bond market comes close to matching this scale. German government bonds are backed by strict fiscal rules, making their supply far too small. Japanese government bonds are almost entirely locked inside the Bank of Japan. Only Washington provides a twenty-seven-trillion-dollar pool of debt deep enough to absorb hundreds of billions of dollars without crashing its exchange rate.

According to the Bank for International Settlements, the dollar remains on one side of more than eighty-eight percent of all foreign exchange transactions. The system does not buy American debt merely because it trusts American politicians. The system buys American debt because modern finance cannot function without this deep reservoir of safe assets. American debt is the structural asset of the rest of the world.
