# Research Vault — Note 05: The Strait of Malacca & The Geoeconomic Pacific Pivot

## Metadata & Executive Summary
- **Topic:** Maritime Volume of the Strait of Malacca, The Chinese "Malacca Dilemma", and Southeast Asian Maritime Security.
- **Source Agencies:** International Maritime Organization (IMO), U.S. Naval War College, Center for Strategic and International Studies (CSIS), Singapore Maritime and Port Authority (MPA).
- **Core Mechanism:** The Strategic Vulnerability — Connecting the Indian and Pacific Oceans, Malacca carries 25% of all global trade and 16 million barrels of oil per day, serving as the physical lever over East Asian manufacturing economies.

---

## 1. Geographic & Navigation Dimensions
- **Location:** 500-mile-long (800 km) narrow funnel between the Malay Peninsula (Malaysia/Singapore) and the Indonesian island of Sumatra.
- **Critical Chokepoint:** The **Phillips Channel** in the Singapore Strait:
  - Narrowest width: **Only 1.5 nautical miles (2.8 kilometers)** wide.
  - Creates a severe natural navigation bottleneck with water depths under 25 meters, limiting maximum draft for Ultra-Large Crude Carriers (Malaccamax limit: 300,000 DWT).

---

## 2. Verified Trade & Energy Volumes
- **Maritime Traffic:**
  - Over **84,000 to 95,000 commercial vessels** transit the strait annually.
  - Carries approximately **25% of all global seaborne merchandise trade**.
- **Petroleum Flows (EIA Data):**
  - Carries approximately **15.0 to 16.0 million barrels per day** of crude oil and petroleum products (second only to Hormuz).
- **The "Malacca Dilemma" for Beijing:**
  - Coined by Chinese President Hu Jintao in November 2003.
  - **~75%–80% of China’s total maritime crude oil imports** transit through the Strait of Malacca from the Middle East and Africa.
  - In addition, **over 60% of China’s total foreign trade by volume** traverses this corridor.
  - Japan and South Korea are equally exposed, with **~80% of Japan’s crude** and **~70% of South Korea’s crude** transiting the strait.

---

## 3. The Structural Bypass Corridors & Their Hard Limits
China has invested over $100 billion through the Belt and Road Initiative (BRI) to construct alternative bypass routes, but physical throughput remains negligible compared to maritime volume:
1. **Myanmar-China Oil & Gas Pipeline (Maday Island to Yunnan):**
   - Crude capacity: **440,000 bpd** (equals ~3% of China's oil imports). Plagued by civil conflict and rebel attacks across northern Myanmar.
2. **China-Pakistan Economic Corridor (CPEC - Gwadar to Xinjiang):**
   - Highly impractical for bulk crude: requires traversing the 15,000-foot Khunjerab Pass across the Himalayas. Freight costs by truck or pipeline would exceed crude value.
3. **Russian ESPO & Central Asian Pipelines:**
   - Russia’s ESPO pipeline supplies ~700,000 to 1,000,000 bpd directly to northern China, valuable but incapable of replacing 10M+ bpd of seaborne imports.
4. **The Kra Isthmus Canal Myth (Thailand):**
   - Repeatedly proposed $30B canal across southern Thailand; blocked due to Thai national security concerns (severing the southern Muslim-majority provinces) and astronomical dredging costs.

---

## 4. The Geoeconomic Balance of Power
- The U.S. Navy's Seventh Fleet, operating out of Yokosuka (Japan) and deploying rotational littoral combat ships from Changi Naval Base (Singapore), maintains undisputed over-the-horizon naval dominance across the eastern approaches to the Strait of Malacca.
- As long as Washington maintains maritime interdiction capability over Malacca, it holds a physical off-switch on China's industrial energy supply, neutralizing Beijing's economic leverage without firing a single missile.
