In its 2022 National Defense Strategy, the Pentagon codified a doctrine known as Integrated Deterrence. The core concept is clear. The United States must synchronize military capabilities, economic influence, and financial leverage across all domains of competition.

When the coercive power of the financial system began to erode, this doctrine pivoted toward the physical arteries of the global economy. And no artery on earth carries more strategic weight than the Strait of Hormuz.

Connecting the Persian Gulf to the Arabian Sea, this passage measures just twenty-one nautical miles across at its narrowest point. The actual shipping lanes in each direction are barely two miles wide. Yet through this narrow channel flows between 20.5 and 21 million barrels of crude oil and petroleum products every single day. That represents more than twenty percent of all liquid petroleum consumed on earth, and nearly thirty percent of all seaborne traded oil.

For half a century, the United States Fifth Fleet, headquartered in Bahrain, has patrolled these waters. Carrier strike groups and guided-missile destroyers maintain continuous surveillance over commercial tankers.

On the surface, this appears to be a mission of national security. But when you examine trade data from the United States Energy Information Administration, an astonishing geopolitical asymmetry emerges.

The United States barely consumes oil from the Persian Gulf anymore. Driven by the domestic shale revolution, America has become a net energy exporter. United States crude imports from Gulf producers have fallen below five hundred thousand barrels per day.

So where does the oil flowing through Hormuz actually go? Over eighty-two percent of it moves eastward toward Asia. It powers the industrial economies of China, India, Japan, and South Korea. China alone imports several million barrels of Middle Eastern crude through this waterway every single day.

Why would the United States Navy spend hundreds of millions of dollars each year safeguarding the energy lifeline of China and other Asian competitors?

The answer lies in the total absence of physical alternatives. Major energy exporters have constructed bypass pipelines, such as Saudi Arabia's East-West Petroline and the Abu Dhabi Crude Oil Pipeline. But these overland routes have a combined capacity of less than four million barrels per day.

If the Strait of Hormuz were closed, seventeen million barrels of oil per day would be completely stranded inside the Gulf. That deficit amounts to nearly one-fifth of global oil consumption. It would trigger an immediate supply shock that no strategic reserve could absorb.

The United States does not patrol Hormuz to protect American consumers at the pump. It patrols Hormuz because controlling that waterway gives Washington sovereign leverage over the entire manufacturing heartland of Eurasia.

By guaranteeing the security of Gulf maritime transit, Washington preserves its role as the ultimate gatekeeper of global trade. In exchange for keeping the world's primary energy valve open, Washington demands that the oil flowing through it continues to be invoiced in United States dollars.

Control of Hormuz is not an energy policy. It is the physical enforcement mechanism that protects the sovereign debt machine of the United States.

Yet once that energy successfully navigates the Persian Gulf, it must cross the Indian Ocean and enter a second, even narrower maritime bottleneck. And it is there that the physical limits of American naval power are being tested like never before.
