The Great Automotive Realignment: BYD’s Expansion, Price Wars, and the Reshaping of the Southeast Asian EV Ecosystem
The Volatility of the Thai Electric Vehicle Sector (2024–2026)
The automotive landscape in Southeast Asia is undergoing a structural realignment as Chinese New Energy Vehicle (NEV) manufacturers aggressively expand their presence, disrupting decades of Japanese dominance [cite: 1, 2, 3]. Thailand has emerged as the primary operational anchor and right-hand-drive export hub for this transition, led by the rapid market entry of BYD [cite: 1, 4].
The transition has been characterized by dramatic macroeconomic fluctuations. In 2024, the total Thai automotive market contracted by 14.4% to 690,890 units due to high household debt, weak domestic purchasing power, and stringent lending criteria enforced by financial institutions [cite: 5, 6]. Despite this overall contraction, the market share of Zero-Emission Vehicles (ZEVs) rose to 10.87%, driven by 70,137 pure electric vehicle registrations [cite: 1, 4]. BYD established undisputed leadership in the EV segment during 2024, registering 27,005 passenger electric vehicles and capturing 38.5% of the electric vehicle market share [cite: 4].

In 2025, total vehicle sales in Thailand fell further to 591,172 units [cite: 5]. However, the electric vehicle segment experienced a strong counter-cyclical rebound, rising 56.2% to 113,719 units and capturing 18% of the total vehicle market [cite: 5, 6]. BYD consolidated its presence, delivering 44,666 vehicles globally to the Thai market, which elevated its share to 6.9% of the total passenger car market, ranking it fourth overall behind Toyota, Honda, and Isuzu [cite: 6, 7].
This momentum culminated in an unprecedented surge in January 2026, when new light vehicle sales rose 41.9% year-on-year to 90,283 units [cite: 8]. This spike was driven by a rush of consumers seeking to register vehicles before the transition from the government's EV 3.0 subsidy scheme to the less lucrative EV 3.5 program [cite: 1, 8, 9]. During this month, BYD registered a record 12,791 units, capturing a historic 14.2% of the total Thai automotive market and placing second overall behind Toyota [cite: 8]. The compact Dolphin hatchback led this surge, leaping to the third position among all vehicle models with a 6.5% total market share [cite: 8].
However, this policy-driven demand proved fragile. In February 2026, registrations collapsed, with electric mobility volumes falling 22.1% year-on-year to 5,744 units [cite: 1]. The expiration of the EV 3.0 subsidies triggered massive sticker shock as manufacturers raised retail prices [cite: 9]. The price of the BYD Dolphin jumped 33% from 789,000 THB to 1.05 million THB, the Atto 3 rose 10.5%, and the MG4 increased by 8% [cite: 9]. Consequently, the combined market share of Chinese EV brands plummeted from 47.3% in January to 11.6% in February [cite: 9].
This sudden contraction exposed deep structural issues within the localized supply chain. Neta (Hozon Auto) was forced to freeze its local operations entirely due to asset freezes faced by its parent company in China [cite: 9]. Simultaneously, public charging infrastructure failed to keep pace with cumulative vehicle sales, causing the vehicle-to-charger ratio to deteriorate from 25:1 to 45:1 within a single year, exacerbating consumer range anxiety [cite: 9].

The Dynamics of the Thai EV Price War: Backlash, Regulation, and the Lemon Law
The volatility in retail pricing stems from a substantial supply-demand mismatch [cite: 1]. Under the EV 3.0 scheme, foreign manufacturers were required to offset imported completely built-up (CBU) vehicles with localized assembly at a 1:1 ratio, which rose to 1.5:1 under subsequent extensions [cite: 10, 11]. This policy forced Chinese OEMs into an aggressive "industrial capture mode," rapidly building local assembly plants to avoid penalties [cite: 1, 10]. BYD constructed a 150,000-unit annual capacity factory in Rayong, Great Wall Motor expanded to 14,500 units, GAC Aion built a 50,000-unit facility, and Changan invested 10 billion THB in its Rayong base [cite: 1]. By 2026, the combined annual manufacturing capacity of Chinese OEMs in Thailand exceeded 250,000 vehicles, far outstripping domestic absorption capabilities, which stood at roughly 70,582 registrations in 2024 and 113,719 in 2025 [cite: 1, 5].
To clear inventory and satisfy government assembly quotas, manufacturers engaged in a highly aggressive price war [cite: 10, 11]. In mid-2024, BYD's sole distributor, Rever Automotive, implemented steep discount campaigns, reducing the retail price of the premium BYD Seal sedan by up to 340,000 THB ($9,400) and cutting the Atto 3 price by 15.6% [cite: 11, 12, 13]. While these discounts drove short-term retail sales, they triggered severe backlash from early adopters [cite: 11, 12]. Consumers who had purchased the Atto 3 at its original list price of 1.19 million THB were outraged to find the identical model retailing for 859,000 THB just months later [cite: 12].
Aggrieved owners publicly protested on social media and at dealerships, with some vandalizing their own vehicles in frustration [cite: 12, 14]. The issue escalated to the Prime Minister’s Office, where Prime Minister Srettha Thavisin personally urged BYD CEO Wang Chuanfu to establish price-protection policies to shield consumer equity [cite: 12, 13]. In response, Rever Automotive launched a compensatory cash-back program, offering existing owners up to 50,000 THB ($1,382) in charging credits through March 2025 [cite: 13].
Simultaneously, the Office of the Consumer Protection Board (OCPB) launched an investigation into Rever Automotive's sales practices following allegations that sales representatives had misled buyers into believing vehicle prices would rise after promotional events, only to slash them further [cite: 12, 14, 15]. By late 2024, the OCPB cleared BYD of illegal discounting—ruling that actual price cuts do not violate advertising laws—but fined Rever Automotive and one executive a total of $3,498 for failing to disclose the exact retail values of promotional giveaways [cite: 15]. Furthermore, the Trade Competition Commission warned that selling products below cost without justifiable business grounds could constitute a violation of the Trade Competition Act, though transitioning inventory for outgoing models was deemed legally permissible [cite: 12].
To address these systemic vulnerabilities, the Thai government accelerated the development of a comprehensive consumer protection framework [cite: 16, 17]. On June 16, 2026, the Cabinet approved the draft Liability for Defective Goods Act, commonly referred to as the "Lemon Law" [cite: 17, 18]. The bill was subsequently approved in principle by the House of Representatives on June 25, 2026, with a unanimous 420 to 0 vote, and was referred to a 24-member special committee for scrutiny [cite: 18, 19].

The draft Lemon Law establishes clear remedies for consumers: product repair, direct replacement, price reduction, or contract termination with a full refund [cite: 17, 21]. By shifting the burden of proof from the buyer to the seller, the law mandates that any defect occurring within one year for motor vehicles is legally presumed to have existed at delivery [cite: 19, 20]. Sellers are required to resolve repairs within 90 days for automobiles, failing which the consumer can immediately initiate contract termination or demand a new vehicle of the same model [cite: 17, 21]. The legislation applies to business-to-consumer and business-to-business transactions, including hire-purchase and credit-financed sales, but explicitly excludes peer-to-peer sales and secondhand vehicles [cite: 20, 21].
Used EV Market Mechanics: Residual Value Erosion and Technological Obsolescence
A highly damaging consequence of the ongoing price war is the severe depreciation and near-collapse of secondhand EV residual values in Thailand [cite: 16, 22]. According to data compiled by Krungsri Research and leading used-car platforms, the resale value of used BEVs in Thailand declines at a rate far exceeding both historical ICE vehicle averages and global EV benchmarks [cite: 22].
The rapid depreciation of electric vehicles is driven by a structural combination of new-car price cuts, range advancements, and concerns over battery degradation [cite: 16, 22, 23]. Used electric passenger cars take on average one-third longer to resell than their conventional ICE counterparts [cite: 23]. In response to this high depreciation and elevated household debt, the average vehicle ownership period in Thailand has lengthened from seven to ten years as consumers delay upgrading their vehicles to avoid negative equity [cite: 24].

This steep depreciation curve varies significantly by brand origin [cite: 22]. Premium European and American BEVs hold their resale value better than Chinese imports, demonstrating a 19.2% and 17.6% retention advantage, respectively, over Chinese marques [cite: 22]. Conversely, Japanese, British, and South Korean BEVs underperform Chinese models, exhibiting worse depreciation by margins of -23.6%, -10.0%, and -25.0% respectively compared to Chinese benchmarks [cite: 22].
The rapid loss of value has severely undermined buyer confidence, causing many consumers to postpone purchases [cite: 10, 11]. To stabilize the market, financial technology firms and insurance providers are exploring guaranteed buyback programs and residual-value insurance policies [cite: 10]. Additionally, data analytics firms are building pricing intelligence platforms to map real-time depreciation trends and assist dealers in pricing used EV stock [cite: 10].
The Eclipse of Japanese Dominance: Structural Retreat and Supply Chain Realignment
For over sixty years, Japanese automakers maintained an undisputed oligopoly in Southeast Asia, historically capturing over 90% of the market share in Thailand and Indonesia [cite: 2, 3, 25]. However, the entry of subsidized, vertically integrated Chinese EV manufacturers has broken this stronghold, resulting in a dramatic contraction of Japanese market share [cite: 3, 10, 25].
By late 2025, the combined Thai market share of nine major Japanese automakers fell to 69.8%, a significant decline from 77.8% in 2023 [cite: 3]. In Indonesia, which represents approximately 30% of the regional automotive market, the Japanese share fell below 90% in 2024 and dropped further to 82.9% by late 2025 [cite: 3]. Confronted with a permanent contraction in ICE and eco-car demand, Japanese automakers have been forced to execute a series of historic factory closures and downsizings [cite: 3, 26]:
Subaru (TCSAT): In May 2024, Tan Chong International Limited (TCIL) and Subaru Corporation announced the complete cessation of vehicle assembly operations at the Tan Chong Subaru Automotive Thailand (TCSAT) joint venture plant in Bangkok's Lat Krabang district [cite: 27, 28, 29]. The 100,000-unit capacity plant, which represented a 5 billion baht investment, will end assembly by December 2024 after years of declining sales and losses, transitioning entirely to importing completely built-up (CBU) units from Japan starting in 2025 [cite: 27, 28, 30, 31]. The closure comes amid financial difficulties at parent company Tan Chong Motor Holdings, which recorded four consecutive years of losses, including a net loss of RM129 million in its last fiscal year [cite: 27, 28].
Suzuki (SMT): Suzuki Motor Corporation decided to permanently close its automobile plant in Pluakdaeng, Rayong, by the end of 2025 [cite: 32]. Suzuki Motor (Thailand) had operated since 2012, producing eco-cars like the Swift, Ciaz, and Celerio [cite: 32]. However, Suzuki's eco-car production plummeted from a peak of 8,000 units per month to under 1,000 units per month, resulting in a net loss of 264 million baht on revenues of 7.03 billion baht in 2023 [cite: 33]. The plant, which employed approximately 800 workers, produced just 7,579 units in FY23 [cite: 32]. Suzuki will transition to importing CBUs from regional hubs in Japan, India, and other ASEAN countries [cite: 32].
Honda Motor: In July 2024, Honda announced a drastic restructuring of its production footprint, cutting its Thai vehicle assembly capacity by over 50% from 270,000 to 120,000 vehicles per year [cite: 34]. Honda will cease vehicle assembly at its historical Ayutthaya plant (opened in 1996) by the end of 2025, transitioning the facility entirely to automotive component manufacturing [cite: 26, 34]. Finished vehicle assembly will be consolidated at its newer Prachinburi plant, which will prioritize hybrid vehicle assembly and EV production, such as the HR-V-based e:N1 EV [cite: 26, 35].

These factory closures present a severe threat to regional supply chains, which are home to more than 2,700 Japanese parts manufacturers [cite: 3]. Compounding these regional shifts, global automotive supply chains faced severe disruptions in July 2026 when a 7.1-magnitude earthquake struck Kumamoto Prefecture in Kyushu, Japan [cite: 36, 37]. The earthquake damaged transport infrastructure and disrupted logistics networks, forcing Toyota to suspend operations at three major southern Japan assembly facilities (Miyata, Kanda, and Kokura plants) and prompting Honda to halt production at its Kumamoto motorcycle plant [cite: 36, 37]. Semiconductor manufacturers Renesas Electronics, TSMC, and Sony also suspended operations in Kumamoto to assess equipment damage, highlighting the supply chain vulnerabilities of legacy Japanese automotive networks [cite: 36, 37].
Regulatory Stepping Stones: Indonesia's TKDN Framework and National Brand Incentives
In Indonesia, which represents approximately 30% of the total Southeast Asian automotive market, the government is executing a highly structured, regulatory-driven strategy to establish a regional EV manufacturing and battery supply chain hub [cite: 3, 38, 39]. The primary mechanism driving this localization is the Tingkat Komponen Dalam Negeri (TKDN), or Domestic Component Level framework [cite: 40, 41].
The Indonesian government has historically linked lucrative fiscal incentives, such as the Value-Added Tax Borne by Government (PPN DTP), to TKDN thresholds [cite: 38, 39, 42]. Under Ministry of Finance Regulation No. 12 of 2025, the government continues to offer a 10% VAT deduction (reducing the consumer's VAT burden to just 1%) for battery electric vehicles (KBLBB) that achieve a TKDN of ≥40% [cite: 38, 39, 42]. For vehicles with a TKDN between 20% and 40%, the government offers a 5% VAT deduction, leaving a 6% payable VAT rate [cite: 38, 39, 42].
Recognizing that achieving a 60% TKDN requirement was impossible without a mature domestic battery manufacturing ecosystem, the government formally postponed its localization deadlines [cite: 43, 44]:
TKDN 40% Requirement: Postponed from 2024 to 2026 to allow the domestic battery ecosystem to mature [cite: 43, 44].
TKDN 60% Requirement: Postponed from 2024 to 2027–2029 [cite: 43, 44].
TKDN 80% Requirement: Standardized for 2030 and beyond [cite: 43, 44].
This delay is a pragmatic acknowledgment of supply chain realities, as battery packs constitute 40% to 50% of an EV's production cost [cite: 43, 44]. By coordinating the TKDN timeline with the commercial readiness of domestic battery manufacturing, which is scheduled to scale by 2026, the government has established a more realistic compliance path for foreign OEMs [cite: 43, 44].
To streamline administrative burdens, the Ministry of Industry issued Regulation No. 35 of 2025 (Permenperin 35/2025), which overhauled the calculation of domestic content [cite: 45, 46]. Rather than relying solely on cost-allocation ratios, the updated framework utilizes a factor-weighted component-based approach across direct materials (weighted at 75%), direct labor (weighted at 10%), and factory overhead (weighted at 15%) [cite: 46].

The Emerging Philippine EV Frontier: Strategic Fiscal Incentives and Geopolitical Dichotomies
Unlike Thailand and Indonesia, which possess established automotive manufacturing bases, the Philippines has historically operated as an import-driven market, relying on CBU imports from Thailand, Indonesia, and China [cite: 49, 50]. However, the country is executing a rapid transition, catalyzed by high domestic fuel prices and targeted legislative support [cite: 50, 51]. The domestic transport sector has shown strong organic demand for electrification, with commuters and fleet operators adopting electric jeepneys (e-jeepneys) as fuel prices remain elevated [cite: 49, 51].
In the first half of 2026, electric vehicle sales in the Philippines surged 132.7% to 31,381 units [cite: 51]. In contrast, sales of traditional internal combustion engine (ICE) vehicles declined 11.4% to 204,557 units over the same period [cite: 51]. To capture this momentum and transition from an import-dependent consumer into a regional manufacturing hub, President Ferdinand Marcos Jr. approved the landmark Electric Vehicle Incentive Strategy (EVIS) Program under Executive Order (EO) No. 121, signed on July 29, 2026 [cite: 51, 52].
The EVIS program allocates 60 billion PHP (approximately $1 billion) in fiscal support over a ten-year horizon, targeting both the startup and production phases [cite: 51, 52]. The administration of the program is highly structured [cite: 51]:
Program Structure: Four total assembly slots are available [cite: 51].
Model Enrollment: Enrolled car manufacturers are permitted to register up to two EV models [cite: 51].
Funding Ceilings: Each enrolled EV model can receive up to 15 billion PHP in total fiscal support [cite: 51].
Incentive Format: Provided in the form of non-transferable Tax Payment Certificates, which manufacturers can use to settle national government obligations, including income tax, VAT, excise tax, and import duties [cite: 51].
The financial support is divided into two operational phases [cite: 51, 53]:
Startup Cost Incentives: Covers up to 40% of eligible startup capital expenditures for manufacturing battery electric vehicles (BEVs) and up to 30% for hybrid (HEV), plug-in hybrid (PHEV), and fuel-cell EV assembly lines [cite: 51, 53].
Production Volume Incentives: Offers a production volume incentive (PVI) of up to 12% of the ex-factory unit price, capped at 200,000 PHP ($3,400) per vehicle [cite: 51, 53]. This incentive is available for up to 10 years, provided the manufacturer commits to locally producing complete units or critical components and maintains a minimum planned production capacity of 10,000 units [cite: 51, 53].
To qualify for the EVIS program, applicants must meet strict criteria, including a minimum capital investment of 5 billion PHP ($85 million) and a commitment to launch their enrolled EV models in the domestic or export market within three years of registration [cite: 51, 52]. Additionally, manufacturers must guarantee a minimum of 10 years of after-sales support, including spare parts availability and battery recycling protocols [cite: 51].

This regulatory push contrasts with geopolitical realities in the region [cite: 49, 50]. Despite ongoing military and diplomatic tensions between the Philippines and China in the South China Sea, the Philippine EV market remains heavily reliant on imported Chinese vehicles and components [cite: 49, 50]. Chinese brands, led by BYD, control the majority of the market due to zero-tariff agreements on BEVs and components extended through 2028 [cite: 49, 50]. In 2024, BYD achieved an extraordinary 8,900% sales growth in the country, capturing 69% of the battery electric vehicle market [cite: 50].
This reliance on Chinese imports is driven by the lack of a local automotive manufacturing base, forcing the Philippines to depend on China's massive cost competitiveness, battery manufacturing capabilities, and critical mineral processing chains [cite: 50]. While the EVIS program aims to establish domestic assembly, Chinese imports remain the most affordable and practical options for the immediate future [cite: 50]. However, legacy Japanese manufacturers, such as Mitsubishi, are actively seeking to leverage the EVIS program to establish localized hybrid assembly, creating a competitive dynamic with Chinese brands [cite: 49, 53].
Conclusions and Outlook
The transition toward electric mobility in Southeast Asia has evolved from a progressive environmental policy into a fierce geopolitical and industrial contest [cite: 1, 40]. As Chinese manufacturers like BYD rapidly deploy vertically integrated supply chains and establish major regional manufacturing bases, legacy Japanese brands are facing a structural crisis [cite: 1, 3, 26]. This shift has already resulted in historic factory closures and production consolidations across Thailand [cite: 3, 26, 27].
The volatility in the Thai EV market highlights a key challenge: rapid industrial expansion without corresponding demand-side stability can trigger destabilizing market feedback loops [cite: 10]. The resulting collapse of used EV residual values and subsequent consumer backlash demonstrate that a sustainable transition requires robust financial infrastructure, transparent battery health metrics, and comprehensive consumer protection laws, such as the emerging Thai Lemon Law [cite: 10, 16, 17].
Regionally, a fragmented regulatory landscape is unfolding [cite: 40]. Thailand is pursuing an "industrial capture" strategy, Indonesia is enforcing strict TKDN local-content requirements linked to domestic battery production, and the Philippines is deploying a $1 billion incentive program to capture assembly investments [cite: 1, 39, 43, 51]. Navigating these diverse frameworks requires automotive manufacturers to develop localized compliance strategies, secure regional supply chains, and balance pricing with brand equity protection to capture long-term growth in the Southeast Asian market [cite: 40, 45].
