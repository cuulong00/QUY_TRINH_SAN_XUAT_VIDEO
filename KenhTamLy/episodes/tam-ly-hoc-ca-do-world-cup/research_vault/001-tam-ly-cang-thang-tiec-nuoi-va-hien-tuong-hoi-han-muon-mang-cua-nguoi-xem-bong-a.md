### [2] luke_clark_2009.txt

> Study Title: Gambling Near-Misses Enhance Motivation to Gamble and Activate Brain Reward Systems Authors: Luke Clark, Antony J. Lawrence, Florence Astley-Jones, and Adrian D. Gray Journal: Neuron (2009), Volume 61, Issue 4, Pages 645-652. Abstract & Key Findings: A near-miss is an outcome in a game of chance that falls close to a winning combination, but is objectively a loss. Near-misses are widely believed to encourage continued play, but their neural basis has not been investigated in detail. In this study, using fMRI, the researchers measured brain activity during a gambling task. The results demonstrated that near-misses (e.g. reel spins on a slot machine that stop one position away from a winning line, or sporting events lost at the final minute) significantly increase the self-reported motivation to gamble. Brain imaging (fMRI) showed that near-misses recruit the same reward-related circuitry that is activated by actual wins. Specifically, near-misses triggered strong activation in the ventral striatum and the anterior insula. The ventral striatum is a key component of the mesolimbic dopamine pathway, which signals anticipation and motivation. The activation in the insula was found to correlate with subjective ratings of near-misses and with the participant's overall gambling severity. Importantly, the near-miss effect was modulated by personal control. When participants had personal agency (e.g., they chose the play or stopped the reels themselves), near-misses triggered significantly stronger activation in the reward system compared to when the machine chose. This provides a neurobiological explanation for the "illusion of control" and explains why gamblers persist during losing streaks: their brains process near-misses as "unfinished success" rather than losses, reinforcing the behavior through dopamine release.
