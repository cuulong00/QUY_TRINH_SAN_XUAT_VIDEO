### [2] luke_clark_2009.txt

> Study Title: Gambling Near-Misses Enhance Motivation to Gamble and Activate Brain Reward Systems Authors: Luke Clark, Antony J. Lawrence, Florence Astley-Jones, and Adrian D. Gray Journal: Neuron (2009), Volume 61, Issue 4, Pages 645-652. Abstract & Key Findings: A near-miss is an outcome in a game of chance that falls close to a winning combination, but is objectively a loss. Near-misses are widely believed to encourage continued play, but their neural basis has not been investigated in detail. In this study, using fMRI, the researchers measured brain activity during a gambling task. The results demonstrated that near-misses (e.g. reel spins on a slot machine that stop one position away from a winning line, or sporting events lost at the final minute) significantly increase the self-reported motivation to gamble. Brain imaging (fMRI) showed that near-misses recruit the same reward-related circuitry that is activated by actual wins. Specifically, near-misses triggered strong activation in the ventral striatum and the anterior insula. The ventral striatum is a key component of the mesolimbic dopamine pathway, which signals anticipation and motivation. The activation in the insula was found to correlate with subjective ratings of near-misses and with the participant's overall gambling severity. Importantly, the near-miss effect was modulated by personal control. When participants had personal agency (e.g., they chose the play or stopped the reels themselves), near-misses triggered significantly stronger activation in the reward system compared to when the machine chose. This provides a neurobiological explanation for the "illusion of control" and explains why gamblers persist during losing streaks: their brains process near-misses as "unfinished success" rather than losses, reinforcing the behavior through dopamine release.

> 10.1016/j.nicl.2014.06.008 Item in Clipboard Full text links Cite Display options Display options Format Abstract PubMed PMID Abstract Frontostriatal circuitry is implicated in the cognitive distortions associated with gambling behaviour. 'Near-miss' events, where unsuccessful outcomes are proximal to a jackpot win, recruit overlapping neural circuitry with actual monetary wins. Personal control over a gamble (e.g., via choice) is also known to increase confidence in one's chances of winning (the 'illusion of control'). Using psychophysiological interaction (PPI) analyses, we examined changes in functional connectivity as regular gamblers and non-gambling participants played a slot-machine game that delivered wins, near-misses and full-misses, and manipulated personal control. We focussed on connectivity with striatal seed regions, and associations with gambling severity, using voxel-wise regression. For the interaction term of near-misses (versus full-misses) by personal choice (participant-chosen versus computer-chosen), ventral striatal connectivity with the insula, bilaterally, was positively correlated with gambling severity. In addition, some effects for the contrast of wins compared to all non-wins were observed at an uncorrected (p < .001) threshold: there was an overall increase in connectivity between the striatal seeds and left orbitofrontal cortex and posterior insula, and a negative correlation for gambling severity with the connectivity between the right ventral striatal seed and left anterior cingulate cortex. These findings corroborate the 'non-categorical' nature of reward processing in gambling: near-misses and full-misses are objectively identical outcomes that are processed differentially. Ventral striatal connectivity with the insula correlated positively with gambling severity in the illusion of control contrast, which could be a risk factor for the cognitive distortions and loss-chasing that are characteristic of problem gambling.
